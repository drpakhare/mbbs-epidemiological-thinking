Epidemiological thinking: counting, dividing and comparing, told through stories

AIIMS Bhopal · 60 minutes

Good morning. Today is not about memorising formulas. It is about a way of thinking that you will use every day as a doctor, whenever someone says "this causes that" or "this cures that". We will start with newspaper headlines, then go back in time to a few doctors who worked this out before microbes were known. Along the way we will build one simple tool, the 2 by 2 table, and learn to read the numbers that come out of it. There will be questions for you on many slides. Please answer them before I show the next slide.

Same vegetable, opposite headlines. A story is not a rate

"Lauki is good for you"

Based on tradition and testimonials. No count of people who drank it and how they fared.

"Lauki juice killed a scientist"

One death in Delhi, 2011, after drinking bitter bottle gourd juice. An expert committee then advised: do not drink juice that tastes bitter.

One case tells you something can happen. It cannot tell you how often, or whether it happens more than without lauki.

To know how often, you need a denominator. To know "more than", you need a comparison.

Indian J Med Res 2012;135:49-55 (ICMR study of bitter bottle gourd juice toxicity).

Both headlines are about the same vegetable. The second one was real. In 2011 a senior CSIR official in Delhi died after drinking bitter lauki juice. An ICMR committee studied the cases. The toxin is cucurbitacin, which makes the gourd bitter. Their advice was simple: if the juice tastes bitter, do not drink it. Notice what a single case gives you: it tells you the harm is possible. It does not tell you how common it is. For that you need a denominator, how many people drank it. And to say lauki is better or worse, you need a comparison group. Denominator and comparison: these two words are the whole of today's session.

Your patients will ask you the same kind of question

A 51-year-old man has chest tightness for two weeks, after meals and walking uphill. It settles with rest. He stopped smoking three years ago. BP 150/96. ECG normal. He lost his job six months ago.

Am I sick?

How sure are you?

None of these can be answered from this one patient alone.

Case adapted from Fletcher RH, Fletcher SW, Fletcher GS. Clinical Epidemiology: The Essentials, 5th ed.

Now think like a doctor, not a newspaper reader. This man is from the Fletchers' textbook of clinical epidemiology. Listen to his questions. Am I sick? How sure are you? What is causing it? What will happen to me? What can be done, and at what cost? Every one of these is a question about groups of people like him. How often does chest pain like this turn out to be angina? How good is a stress test? How much does stopping smoking reduce his risk? You answer each of them with numbers that came from studying many patients.

Epidemiology: who gets sick, why, and what we do about it

The study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to control health problems.

Distribution

Time, place, person. How often: rates, ratios, proportions.

Determinants

Exposures and risk factors: biological, behavioural, environmental, social.

Application

Prevent, control, treat, plan services. Knowing is not the end.

The core move is always the same: count, divide, compare.

Last JM, ed. A Dictionary of Epidemiology. Oxford University Press.

Here is the formal definition you will write in exams. Three words matter. Distribution: who, where and when, expressed as rates and proportions. Determinants: the exposures that change the chance of disease. Application: we study this to act on it. I want you to remember a shorter version: count, divide, compare. Count the cases. Divide by the people at risk. Compare with a group that was not exposed. Our first story shows what happens when you skip the dividing.

London, 1849: the higher the district, the less cholera

Real data for 38 districts, from William Farr. Ask the room first, then press "Colour by water supply". Open full screen

Before Snow, there was William Farr, the great medical statistician of the General Register Office. After the 1849 epidemic he plotted cholera deaths against the height of each London district above the Thames. Look at it: a smooth, strong gradient. Districts at the river had over 90 deaths per 10,000; districts above 60 feet had about 20. Farr fitted a mathematical law to it and concluded that cholera came from bad air, miasma, lying low near the river. Ask the room: is this convincing? It is strong, it is consistent, it even has a dose-response. Now press "Colour by water supply". The low districts are the ones drinking Thames water drawn in central London, full of sewage. Height and water travel together. Height was a confounder. A dose-response does not protect you from confounding. Farr himself changed his mind after the 1866 epidemic. Snow, five years later, found a way to separate water from everything else.

Snow J. On the Mode of Communication of Cholera, 2nd ed. London; 1855.

John Snow was a London anaesthetist. He even gave chloroform to Queen Victoria. He believed cholera spread through water, when most doctors believed in bad air, miasma. In 1854 two companies supplied water to the same streets of south London. Southwark and Vauxhall took water from the Thames below the sewage outlets. Lambeth had moved its intake upstream. Here are the deaths. Ask the room: the rest of London has the most deaths, so is its water the worst? Wait for someone to say "we need to know how many people each company supplied".

Counts mislead. Divide by the number at risk

A rate needs a denominator. The most deaths is not the highest risk.

Snow went door to door and found out which company supplied each house. Now divide deaths by houses. Southwark and Vauxhall: 315 deaths per 10,000 houses. Lambeth: 37. Rest of London: 55. The rest of London had the most deaths only because it had the most houses. Southwark and Vauxhall houses had about eight times the death rate of Lambeth houses. A small note: Snow's printed table gives 59 for the rest of London; these house and death counts give 55. And he used houses, not people, as the denominator, because that was what he could count. This was called the Grand Experiment: similar people on the same streets, differing mainly in their water.

Broad Street, 1854: put every death on a map

Snow's 578 deaths and 13 pumps. Tick "Colour each death by its nearest pump". Open full screen

The same year, a violent outbreak hit Soho, in central London. Snow marked every death on a street map. Each dot is one death, the triangles are public water pumps. Where do the dots cluster? Tick the box: the dark dots are the deaths nearer the Broad Street pump than any other, about six in ten of all deaths. Snow also found telling exceptions: workers at a brewery nearby, who drank beer and had their own well, were spared; a woman in Hampstead, far away, who had Broad Street water carried to her because she liked its taste, died. Now look at the curve on the right. The pump handle came off on 8 September, but deaths were already falling, partly because people had fled. So the handle removal did not prove anything by itself. The evidence was the map and the comparison. Be honest about this with students; the popular story oversells the handle.

Here is the same comparison arranged as a 2 by 2 table. Exposure goes in the rows: dirty water or clean water. Outcome in the columns: died or did not die. The four cells are always called a, b, c and d, in this order. Cell a, the exposed who got the outcome, is the one people look at first. Take a minute and fill in b and d yourselves from the previous slide, total minus deaths. Almost every cohort study, case-control study and trial that you will read reduces to one or more tables like this.

From this one table we get several numbers, and each answers a different question. Risk in the exposed and unexposed. Relative risk: how many times higher, here 8.4. Risk difference, which older books call attributable risk: how many extra deaths per 10,000 houses, here 278. Attributable fraction: of the deaths in the exposed houses, what share is due to the exposure, here 88 percent. And the odds ratio, 8.6, very close to the relative risk because cholera death was rare, about 3 percent. We will see soon why the odds ratio exists at all. Read the green line aloud. That is what attributable fraction means in plain words, assuming the water was the cause.

Vienna, 1846: two maternity clinics, very different deaths

First Clinic

9.9%mothers died, 1841 to 1846

Second Clinic

Deliveries by midwives

3.9%mothers died, 1841 to 1846

Same hospital, same city, admitted on alternate days. Why?

Semmelweis IP. The Etiology, Concept and Prophylaxis of Childbed Fever. 1861. Yearly figures as tabulated by Loudon, J R Soc Med 2013.

Ignaz Semmelweis was in charge of the First Obstetric Clinic of the Vienna General Hospital. Childbed fever, puerperal sepsis, was killing mothers. Women knew the First Clinic was dangerous; some begged not to be admitted there, and some delivered in the street to avoid it. Women were admitted to the two clinics on alternate days, so the groups were similar. Relative risk 2.6. Ask the room: why? Take their theories. At the time people blamed crowding, bad air, fear caused by the priest's bell, the delivery position, and even the embarrassment of being examined by male students.

Semmelweis tested each idea. Hands from the autopsy room fitted

Ruled out

Crowding, the air, the priest's bell, delivery position. Changing them did not change deaths.

The clue

March 1847: colleague Kolletschka cut by a student's knife during an autopsy. He died with the same findings as the mothers.

The test

Doctors and students came straight from the dissection room. From May 1847: wash hands in chlorinated lime.

Compare groups that differ in one thing, then change that thing and see if the outcome follows.

Semmelweis changed things one at a time. He moved the priest. He changed the delivery position. Nothing happened. Then his friend Jakob Kolletschka, a pathologist, was cut by a student's scalpel during an autopsy and died. His autopsy looked just like the mothers'. The difference between the clinics was the doctors and students, who came straight from dissecting bodies to examining women. Midwives did not do autopsies. So in May 1847 he made everyone wash their hands in chlorinated lime. Notice: nobody knew about bacteria yet. He did not need to know the organism to prevent the disease.

After handwashing, the First Clinic caught up with the midwives

First Clinic 1846: 459 of 4,010 mothers died (11.4%). 1848: 45 of 3,556 (1.3%).

Here are the yearly death rates. The dark line is the doctors' clinic, the grey line the midwives'. Before 1847 the gap is wide every single year. The green line marks handwashing in May 1847. By 1848 the First Clinic is at 1.3 percent, the same as the midwives. This before-and-after comparison, with the midwives' clinic as a control running alongside, is strong evidence. Ask: what else could have changed in 1847? That is always the right question to ask of a before-and-after comparison.

He was right, and he was ignored

Senior doctors rejected the idea that their own hands killed mothers. Semmelweis did not publish for years, would not present at meetings, and insulted his critics.

Evidence

Present it, in writing, where others can check it.

Feasibility

The fix must look doable to those who must do it.

Support

Build professional, community and political backing.

Hand hygiene is still the hardest infection control practice to get right in our own wards.

This story does not end well. Semmelweis's colleagues found it offensive that gentlemen doctors could carry death on their hands. He did not publish his work for over a decade, refused to present at meetings, and wrote angry open letters calling his critics murderers. He lost his post and died in an asylum in 1865. The lesson for epidemiology: being right is not enough. You need evidence others can check, an intervention that looks feasible, and support. And ask yourselves how often you have seen hand rub used between patients on your ward.

Nightingale drew her numbers so no one could ignore them

Her monthly Crimean War data. Switch rose and bars, and the two years. Open full screen

Contrast Semmelweis with Florence Nightingale. She was also fighting doctors and generals. But she counted carefully, calculated death rates per 1,000 soldiers, and drew them. In the first year of the Crimean War, about 14 soldiers died of disease for every one killed by wounds. Those are the big dark wedges. The Sanitary Commission arrived in March 1855 and cleaned the drains, the water and the ventilation. Switch to the second year: the wedges shrink. Now switch to plain bars. Same numbers, easier to read exactly. She chose the rose because it persuaded Parliament. Semmelweis had the right answer and lost; Nightingale had the right answer and presented it so it could not be ignored.

A quick tour of the pioneers from my older slides. Hippocrates told doctors to look at the seasons, the water, and how people lived. Graunt, a shopkeeper, counted deaths in London and saw regular patterns. Lind put twelve scurvy sailors on six treatments, two each, on the same ship at the same time, and the pair on oranges and lemons recovered. That is a small controlled trial. Jenner heard a dairy maid say she could not get smallpox because she had had cowpox. He turned that into a test on the boy James Phipps. Today that experiment would never pass an ethics committee. Every one of them is counting, dividing or comparing.

Lung cancer, 1950: start from the sick, not the smokers

Doll and Hill started with the sick. 649 men with lung cancer, and 649 men in hospital for other illnesses. Then they asked about smoking.

Lung cancerControls

Can you calculate the risk of lung cancer in smokers from this table?

Doll R, Hill AB. Smoking and carcinoma of the lung. BMJ 1950;2:739-748.

Snow followed exposed and unexposed houses forward to see who died. That is a cohort design. But lung cancer takes decades to develop. In 1950 Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill in London did it the other way round. They found men with lung cancer, cases, and men in the same hospitals with other illnesses, controls, and asked both about their smoking. Here are the men. Ask the room: what is the risk of lung cancer in smokers? Wait. Someone should notice that the investigators chose to have 649 cases. The column totals were fixed by design.

When you pick the cases, risk is meaningless. Use the odds ratio

OR =a × db × c=647 × 27622 × 2=14

Why not risk?

647 / (647 + 622) = 51% would be "risk" only because the study chose equal numbers of cases and controls. Choose twice the controls and it halves.

Why the OR works

The odds of smoking in cases vs in controls equals the odds of disease in smokers vs non-smokers. When the disease is rare, OR is close to RR.

Cohort study: risks, RR and OR. Case-control study: OR only.

If you divide 647 by 647 plus 622 you get 51 percent. That is not the risk of lung cancer in smokers. It is 51 percent only because the investigators chose one control per case. With two controls per case it would fall to about a third. So in a case-control study we cannot calculate risk, or relative risk. What we can calculate is the odds ratio, a times d over b times c. Here it is 14. The odds ratio has a useful property: the exposure odds ratio equals the disease odds ratio. And when the disease is rare, like lung cancer, the odds ratio is close to the relative risk. So smokers had roughly fourteen times the risk. Later cohort studies, including Doll and Hill's own study of British doctors, confirmed it.

"Doubles the risk" can mean one extra case in 7,000

October 1995: UK regulators warn that newer contraceptive pills double the risk of blood clots in the legs and lungs.

100%increase in risk. The headline
1 → 2women per 7,000 per year. The absolute numbers
~13,000extra abortions the next year, in England and Wales

Always ask for the absolute risk. A relative risk alone can frighten, or flatter, without informing.

Furedi A. Hum Reprod Update 1999;5:621-6. Gigerenzer G et al. Psychol Sci Public Interest 2007;8:53-96.

This is one of the most expensive misunderstandings of relative risk. In October 1995 the UK Committee on Safety of Medicines warned doctors that third-generation pills doubled the risk of venous thrombosis. The newspapers printed "100 percent increase". Many women stopped the pill abruptly. What did doubling mean? About 1 in 7,000 women a year on the older pills, 2 in 7,000 on the newer ones. One extra case per 7,000 women. The next year there were an estimated 13,000 extra abortions, and pregnancy itself carries a higher clot risk than either pill. Relative risk tells you how strong an association is. Risk difference tells you how much it matters to a patient. Give patients both.

Chocolate and Nobel prizes: correlation 0.79

A 2012 paper in the New England Journal of Medicine plotted chocolate eaten per person against Nobel laureates per 10 million people, for 23 countries.

r = 0.79Switzerland on top, China at the bottom

Ecological

Countries, not people. We do not know that the laureates ate the chocolate.

Confounded

Rich countries buy more chocolate and fund more research.

Reversed?

The author joked that laureates might celebrate with chocolate.

Association is where thinking starts, not where it ends.

Messerli FH. Chocolate consumption, cognitive function, and Nobel laureates. N Engl J Med 2012;367:1562-4.

This paper was partly tongue in cheek, but it was real and it was in the New England Journal. The correlation is strong, 0.79. Ask the class to explain it without chocolate making anyone clever. Three explanations. It is ecological: the data are about countries, not individuals. There is confounding: national wealth drives both chocolate and science funding. And there may be reverse causation. The author himself admitted he ate chocolate daily. Keep these three words: ecological, confounding, reverse causation.

This is a real comparison of two operations for kidney stones. Open surgery, A, and percutaneous nephrolithotomy, B. Look at the small stones: A wins. Large stones: A wins again. Now add them up: B wins. This is called Simpson's paradox. Give the room a minute. The answer is on the next slide. Hint: look at where most of A's patients are.

Doctors gave the harder cases to surgery. That is confounding

Who got A

263 of 350 had large stones, which are harder to treat whichever method you use.

Who got B

270 of 350 had small stones, which do well whichever method you use.

Stone size is linked to the treatment chosen and to the outcome. Mixing the groups hides the true comparison.

A confounder is linked to both exposure and outcome, and is not on the path between them. Compare within its levels.

Confounding Lab: split and merge the groups yourself, with a second example on coffee, smoking and lung cancer.

Most patients who got open surgery had large stones. Most who got PCNL had small stones. Large stones fail more often, whatever you do. So B's overall figure looks good because B was given the easy cases. Stone size is linked to the treatment and to the outcome. That is a confounder. The fix is to compare within groups of the confounder: small with small, large with large. Randomisation solves this at the design stage, because it balances stone size between the arms, including factors we never measured. The confounding lab has this example and another: coffee drinkers seem to get more lung cancer, until you split by smoking.

Hormone therapy: studies said protect, the trial said harm

1980s to 1990s: observational studies

Women who chose hormone therapy after menopause had 40 to 50% less heart disease. Millions were prescribed it to prevent heart attacks.

2002: Women's Health Initiative trial

16,608 women randomised. Heart disease hazard ratio 1.29, breast cancer 1.26. Stopped early for harm.

Women who chose hormones were healthier, richer and more careful about their health to begin with.

The people who choose a treatment are not like the people who do not. Randomisation removes that choice.

Writing Group for the WHI Investigators. JAMA 2002;288:321-33.

This is confounding at the scale of millions of prescriptions. For two decades, observational studies found that women on hormone therapy had much less heart disease. Doctors prescribed it for prevention. Then the Women's Health Initiative randomised over sixteen thousand women to oestrogen plus progestin or placebo. The trial was stopped early. Heart disease was higher, not lower, hazard ratio 1.29. Breast cancer was also higher. What went wrong? The women who chose hormones were healthier to start with: more educated, more exercise, better access to care. This is sometimes called the healthy user effect. Randomisation takes the choice away from the patient and the doctor, so the groups are alike.

1954: the families who said no were different

The Salk vaccine field trial, over 400,000 children randomised. Compare the two designs. Open full screen

The biggest public health experiment of its time. In 1954 the Salk polio vaccine was tested on schoolchildren in the United States. In some areas, children whose parents consented were randomised to vaccine or a salt-water placebo. Vaccinated: 16 paralytic cases per 100,000. Placebo: 57. Relative risk 0.29, so the vaccine prevented about 71 percent of cases. Now look at the children whose parents refused: 36 per 100,000, lower than placebo. Why would unvaccinated children do better than placebo? The refusing families were poorer, and paralytic polio, oddly, hit better-off families more, probably because children in more hygienic homes met the virus later in life. So "vaccinated vs not vaccinated" would have been a confounded comparison. Now click the observed-control design, where second-graders were offered vaccine and first and third graders were the comparison. The effect looks smaller, and we cannot be sure the groups were alike. This is why we randomise.

The planes that did not come back

Second World War. Returning bombers are covered in bullet holes on the wings and body, few on the engines. Where should the armour go?

The obvious answer

Where the holes are.

Abraham Wald's answer

Planes hit in the engines did not return. You are only looking at the survivors. Armour where the holes are missing.

Ask who is missing from the data. A hospital sees only those who reached the hospital.

Mangel M, Samaniego FJ. Abraham Wald's work on aircraft survivability. J Am Stat Assoc 1984;79:259-67. The popular story simplifies his technical memoranda.

Ask the room first: where would you add armour? Most say on the wings, where the holes are. The statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that the sample was only planes that made it back. Planes hit in the engine were missing from the data because they were shot down. So the places without holes are where hits are fatal. Historians say the story has been polished over the years, but the idea is sound. It is called survivorship bias, a kind of selection bias. In medicine: a study done in a tertiary hospital sees patients who survived long enough and could afford to reach it. The patients who died at home are the missing planes.

A positive mammogram. What is the chance of cancer?

1%of women screened have breast cancer
90%of women with cancer test positive
9%of women without cancer also test positive

Your guess: 90%? 81%? 10%? 1%?

Gigerenzer G et al. Psychol Sci Public Interest 2007;8:53-96. 160 gynaecologists were asked this.

Now the same table, used for tests. A woman in her fifties has a positive screening mammogram. She asks you: do I have cancer? Here is what we know. One percent of women screened have cancer. The test picks up 90 percent of them. It is also positive in 9 percent of women who do not have cancer. Take a vote: 90, 81, 10 or 1 percent. When Gerd Gigerenzer asked 160 practising gynaecologists this question, only about one in five chose the right answer. Most chose 90 or 81.

Count 1,000 women and the answer is about 1 in 10

9 cancer, test positive

1 cancer, test negative (missed)

89 no cancer, test positive (false alarm)

901 no cancer, test negative

PPV =99 + 89=9%

When the disease is rare, most positives are false, even with a good test.

Turn percentages into people. Take 1,000 women. 1 percent, 10 women, have cancer. The test catches 90 percent of them: 9 test positive, 1 is missed. 990 do not have cancer. 9 percent of them, about 89, also test positive. So there are 98 positive tests, and only 9 of them are cancers. 9 out of 98 is about 9 percent, roughly 1 in 10. The amber dots are the false alarms, and they swamp the dark ones. Gigerenzer found that after doctors were taught to think in counts like this, called natural frequencies, most of them got it right.

Screening Test Lab: move prevalence and watch PPV change while the test stays the same.

Here is the same information as a 2 by 2 table. Now the rows are the test result and the columns are the true disease status. Sensitivity and specificity read down the columns: among people with disease, how many test positive; among people without, how many test negative. Those are properties of the test. Predictive values read across the rows: among positives, how many have disease. That is what the patient wants to know, and it depends on how common the disease is in the people you test. The same mammogram in a breast clinic, where many women have a lump, has a much higher PPV. The next slide lets you see this.

Try it: same test, different prevalence

Here is the test lab. Start at 1 percent prevalence, as in screening. PPV is about 9 percent. Slide prevalence to 20 percent, a symptomatic clinic. PPV jumps to over 70 percent. The test did not change at all. Now push specificity from 91 to 99 percent at low prevalence and watch the false alarms disappear. This is why we screen high-risk groups, and why a positive screen is followed by a confirmatory test.

Five questions to ask of the next headline

  1. Compared with whom? No comparison group, no conclusion.
  2. Out of how many? A count is not a rate. Ask for the denominator.
  3. How big, in absolute terms? "40% lower" from what, to what?
  4. What else could explain it? Chance, bias, confounding, reverse causation.
  5. What kind of study? Anecdote, ecological, case-control, cohort or randomised trial.

Try them on "Coffee cuts liver cancer risk by 40%". It came from a meta-analysis of mostly case-control and cohort studies: RR 0.60.

Bravi F et al. Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol 2013;11:1413-21.

These are the answers the class gave on slide 2, organised. Compared with whom. Out of how many. How big in absolute terms. What else could explain it. What kind of study. Now use them on the coffee headline. The source was a meta-analysis of 16 observational studies, relative risk 0.60 for liver cell cancer. Compared with non-drinkers. Liver cancer is uncommon, so the absolute difference is small. What else? People with liver disease are often told to stop coffee, which is reverse causation, and coffee drinkers may differ in alcohol and hepatitis. And no trial. So: interesting, plausible, not proof.

Three things to carry out of this room

Count, divide, compare. A story or a count alone proves nothing.

From one 2 by 2 table: RR for strength, risk difference for impact, OR when you started from the cases.

Before believing an association, rule out chance, bias and confounding. Before believing a positive test, ask how common the disease is.

Three things to take away. First, the core habit: count, divide, compare. One case, or a big count, tells you nothing about risk. Second, the 2 by 2 table. Relative risk tells you strength, risk difference tells you how much it matters, and in a case-control study you only get the odds ratio. Third, associations can be produced by chance, bias or confounding, as with hormone therapy. And a positive test in a low-prevalence group is usually a false alarm.
